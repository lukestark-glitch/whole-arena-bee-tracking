Results paper

Whole-arena 3D tracking for a bumblebee camera robot: what we built and what the simulation showed

Luke Stark · 18 November 2026 · Written with Claude

Follows the concept paper of 13 November 2026. Everything here comes from a simulation, not from hardware.

Summary

The concept paper proposed fixed cameras around the flight arena that see every bumblebee, a server that triangulates them and keeps one ID per bee, and a camera robot that is told where the selected bee is. No hardware exists, so I built a software proof of concept: a simulator for bees, cameras and camera nodes, a tracking server with a control window, and an emulator of the camera robot. The two programs talk over UDP with the messages a real system would use. The code is public, has about 9,400 lines of Python and 255 tests, and every run is repeatable from a seed.

I then ran 41 experiment cases with 5 seeds each (205 runs). Main findings: with 16 cameras the tracker locates bees to about 0.4 mm on average (1.0 mm for the 95th percentile) and keeps almost every bee tracked, with up to 5 bees. Eight cameras in the same box also work, but they do not meet the criterion I wrote down before the runs. They swap IDs more often, are less accurate when bees gather near the corners, and are fragile if one of their four nodes fails. No layout below 16 cameras passed. Crowding of 10 to 20 bees is the weak point of the tracker, a wrong or missing calibration breaks it, and the wall markers repair it. The robot numbers depend on guessed motor values and are the least reliable part.

This paper describes what was built, how to run it, how the experiments were done, all results with their limits, and what I would ask Jeremy Fielding and test on real hardware next.

1Where this stands

In the concept paper I described a second, fixed tracking system for Jeremy Fielding's bumblebee camera robot. His robot follows the largest yellow blob it sees, so with several bees in the arena the camera jumps from one bee to another. The idea: cameras mounted around the arena see all bees at once, a server computes each bee's 3D position and ID, an operator chooses one bee, and the robot receives where that bee is, how it moves and how far away it is. The robot keeps its own stereo camera for fine centring and focus.

I do not have the hardware or the robot. So the first goal was a software proof of concept against a simulation with realistic hardware parameters. The questions it should answer are the ones that decide whether the idea is worth building: How accurate is the 3D position? Does the tracker keep one ID per bee, and how many bees can it handle? How many cameras are needed? What happens when a camera node fails, when the network is slow, or when the cameras are not placed exactly where the software thinks? Can the robot keep up if it is given the tracker's output?

This paper reports what was built and what the simulation says. It is a result from a model. It shows that the concept and the software work in principle and where they are weak. It does not prove that the real hardware will behave the same way.

2What was built

The project has two programs and one emulator that talk only over UDP, as the real parts would (Figure 1).

  • Simulator. Moves up to 20 virtual bees in the arena (flying, sitting and walking on the walls), simulates 16 cameras that deliver the pixel position of every bee they see, adds noise, dropouts and network delay per camera node, and sends the same detections messages a real camera node would send. It also serves the simulator window.
  • Tracker. Turns pixels into lines of sight, finds 3D points where several lines meet, rejects ghost points, assigns points to tracks with the Hungarian method, filters each track with a constant-acceleration Kalman filter, and keeps one ID per bee. It serves the control window and sends the selected bee to the robot as a target message.
  • Robot emulator. Rail, tilt and focus axes with speed and acceleration limits from a config file, a stereo search window in the robot's own image, and a cinema camera model with a depth of field. It follows the target and reports its pose.
  • Metrics. The only code that sees the ground truth. It compares the tracker's log with the truth and computes error, ID swaps, ghost tracks, prediction error and how well the robot kept the bee in frame and in focus.

The rules that kept the proof of concept honest are written in the repository: the tracker never imports simulator code and never receives the truth, all times are simulation time supplied by the simulator, every random number comes from one seeded generator, and nothing configurable is hard-coded (arena, cameras, nodes, noise, ports and robot values live in YAML files). The same seed and config give the same run.

The work was done in eight stages, each with acceptance criteria, tests and documentation, in order:

The repository keeps every non-obvious choice in a decisions file (about 100 entries) and the numbers of each stage in a changelog, so a result can be traced to the choice behind it.

3The two windows

Both programs serve a browser window with a 3D view built with three.js. It runs in a normal browser without an installation. I use it on a 2019 Intel MacBook with five bees, which is the practical limit for smooth display.

The simulator window (Figure 2) shows the arena, the camera positions with their view cones, the robot on its rail and the bees. The left panel has the controls: number of bees, maximum speed, pixel noise, dropout, speed of time, pause, seed, the flight model, camera nodes that can be stopped, and a displacement of one camera to test calibration. The wings flap while a bee flies and fold when it sits or walks.

The control window (Figure 3) is what an operator would use. It shows every confirmed track with its ID, a trail and a line that predicts where the bee will be, the selected bee's speed and acceleration, the health of every camera node and, below the 3D view, the cinema picture of the robot and one small panel per tracking camera with the detections of its last message. Clicking a bee or a row in the track list selects it, and the robot then follows it. Detections in the camera panels carry the colour and number of the track that used them, so a point that no track uses is visible at once.

The cinema picture (Figure 5) is a simple model of what the robot's macro camera would see: a 24 mm wide field and a depth of field of 2 mm. A bee outside the depth of field is drawn as a soft disc. With the cautious robot limits this is the common case, which matches the robot numbers in section 6.6. These two values are derived from a 1.5× magnification on a 36 mm sensor and a guessed aperture, not measured.

4How to use it

The repository is github.com/lukestark-glitch/whole-arena-bee-tracking. It needs Python 3.11 or newer, Git and a current browser. The complete and current commands are in docs/setup.md. In short:

4.1 Install and test

git clone https://github.com/lukestark-glitch/whole-arena-bee-tracking.git
cd whole-arena-bee-tracking
python3 -m venv .venv
source .venv/bin/activate
pip install -e ".[dev]"
pytest                      # about one minute

4.2 Run it with the windows

Open two terminals, activate the virtual environment in both, and start the tracker first:

python -m tracker.main                # terminal 1: control window on port 8766
python -m simulator.main              # terminal 2: simulator window on port 8765

Then open http://localhost:8766 and http://localhost:8765 in the browser. Click a bee in the control window to select it, and the robot follows it. The simulator window has the switch between random flight and "Trips to the feeder". Useful options: --config to choose another camera layout, --seed for a repeatable run, --auto-select in the tracker to select the oldest confirmed track by itself, and --duration S to stop after S seconds.

4.3 Other layouts and arenas

python -m tracker.main --config config/cameras-8.yaml     # 8 cameras on 4 nodes
python -m simulator.main --config config/cameras-8.yaml
python -m tracker.main --config config/arena-wall.yaml    # box with an opaque partition
python -m simulator.main --config config/arena-wall.yaml

The files config/cameras-8.yaml, cameras-10.yaml and cameras-12.yaml use cameras of the default layout. The robot's values are in config/robot.yaml, and each value there is tagged stated, video, assumed or measured, so it is clear what is known. Change a value, restart, and the robot behaves differently.

4.4 Measure a run and calibrate

python -m experiments.metrics --tracks logs/tracks.jsonl --duration 60   # terminal 1
python -m tracker.main --duration 60 --auto-select                       # terminal 2
python -m simulator.main --duration 60                                   # terminal 3

python -m tracker.wand_run                          # calibration: terminal 1
python -m simulator.main --wand --miscalibrated     # calibration: terminal 2

The first block prints position error, ID swaps, ghost tracks and the share of time each bee was tracked. The second block lets the simulator carry a virtual LED wand through the arena while the cameras are 1 cm and 2° off, and the tracker estimates the true poses (section 6.5).

4.5 Repeat every experiment of this paper

python -m experiments.batch --estimate     # number of runs and expected time
python -m experiments.batch                 # all 41 cases, seeds 1 to 5, about 20 minutes
python -m experiments.coverage --configs config/default.yaml config/cameras-8.yaml

The batch writes experiments/results/ with one row per run (runs.csv), the mean over the seeds (summary.csv), the verdict against the criterion (criterion.csv) and SVG plots. The same seed gives the same numbers.

5How the experiments were done

Each run puts the simulator, the tracker and the robot emulator in one process, so it repeats exactly, and takes 20 simulated seconds. The first 2 seconds are left out (tracks start up), so 18 seconds are evaluated. Every case is run with seeds 1 to 5, and the tables show the mean over the seeds. The tracker selects a bee by itself (the oldest confirmed track), and the robot follows it. The full batch took 22 minutes on an 8-core laptop.

Base case unless a table says otherwise: 16 cameras on 8 nodes, 10 bees, random flight, pixel noise 0.5 px, dropout 2 %, node delay 8 ± 2 ms, true camera poses and the cautious robot limits of config/robot.yaml. The camera comparison uses 5 bees, because that is the number I use in practice.

5.1 The two flight models

No real bumblebee flight data was available, so there are two guesses.

  • Random flight. Each bee's velocity follows an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with about 1 m/s root-mean-square speed and a direction time of 0.5 s. Bees also land on the walls, walk and take off. This is the default, so earlier results do not change.
  • Trips. Based on Jeremy's video: bees are drawn to light and fly straighter when the arena is lit from the sides, and the colony is connected to the chamber with trained bees flying to the nectar. My assumption, which the video does not state, is that the light runs along the arena, with the feeder at the lit end (x = 0) and the colony entrance at the other end. Each bee then goes through a loop: rest at the entrance, fly to the feeder, feed, fly back. Timings are assumed.

5.2 Metrics

  • Mean and p95 error: distance of each matched track from its bee. ID swaps: times a bee was matched to another track ID than before. Ghost tracks: confirmed tracks that matched no bee in more than half their frames (worst seed). Ghost share: share of all confirmed track-frames that matched no bee.
  • Tracked: share of each bee's frames in which a track matched it (mean over bees, and for the worst bee).
  • Prediction: constant-velocity prediction from the logged state, 17 ms (one frame) and 100 ms ahead, against where the bee really was.
  • In frame / in focus: share of the frames with a selected bee in which it was inside the cinema picture, and inside it and within the 2 mm depth of field. Offset and focus error: mean distance of the followed bee from the camera's optical axis and from its focus plane.

5.3 The criterion for 8 cameras, written down first

Before the runs I wrote down what "good enough" means for a smaller layout, so the verdict could not be adjusted afterwards. At 5 bees, for both flight models, averaged over the seeds: at least 95 % of the volume seen by 3 or more cameras; mean error at most 1 mm and p95 at most 2 mm; no ghost track in any seed; tracked share at least 95 %; ID swaps at most 1.5 times those of 16 cameras; robot time in frame and in focus at most 5 points below 16 cameras. The 8-camera layout has one camera on each corner of the box, all aimed at the centre, on 4 nodes with the two cameras of one vertical edge each.

In plain words.

  • 8 cameras locate bees well but are not "good enough". With random flight they fail one point: ID swaps (2.4 against 0.4 for 16 cameras in 18 s). With trips they fail two: the p95 error is 2.33 mm (limit 2 mm) and the robot is in focus 68 % of the time against 79 % with 16 cameras.
  • Where 8 cameras are weak. Errors and swaps grow with trips, where bees gather at the end walls by the feeder and the entrance, right next to the corner cameras. A camera sees a bee that close at a steep angle and from few directions, and bees close together near a wall are confused more often. The data do not locate the errors in space. "Near the end walls" follows from the trips layout and the coverage maps, not from a measurement.
  • A node failure hurts the 8-camera layout much more. With one of the 4 nodes stopped, a whole vertical edge goes dark. The worst bee is then tracked only 84 % (random) or 63 % (trips) of the time, and the p95 error with trips is 3.4 mm. With 16 cameras, two of 8 nodes stopped still leave the worst bee at 98 %.
  • 10 and 12 cameras do not pass either. 10 cameras fail only ID swaps (random) or only focus (trips). 12 cameras had one ghost track in one random seed and a p95 of 2.01 mm with trips, so 12 is not clearly better than 10 or 8. With 5 seeds of 18 s, a difference of 1 to 2 ID swaps or 0.1 to 0.3 mm of p95 is within the spread between seeds.
  • The smallest number that passes is 16. The criterion is strict where 16 cameras are nearly perfect: 0.4 swaps in 18 s leave little room for 1.5 times.
  • Robot numbers do not separate the layouts. With random flight the robot was in frame 75 % of the time with 8 cameras and 62 % with 16, because the spread between seeds is 13 to 20 points (each seed follows a different bee).

Hardware for 8 cameras

Counts come from the layouts. Prices are rough list prices, assumed and to be checked before buying: a camera with a wide lens about 80 USD, a Raspberry Pi 5 with PoE board, card and case about 100 USD, a PoE switch 60 to 150 USD. The trigger generator is the same in all layouts.

An 8-camera arena halves the cost of the first build. Its p95 error is 0.3 mm higher with random flight and 1.3 mm higher with trips, it swaps IDs more often, and it is fragile when a node fails. Its cameras are 8 of the 16 default positions, so it can be extended to 16 later. My reading: 8 cameras are a sensible first step to see whether the idea works on real hardware, if the first build uses at most a few bees and a good node supply. For a system that has to be reliable with many bees, 16 cameras are the safer plan.

6.6 The robot

Only the rail speed of 3 m/s and the use of NEMA 23 stepper motors are known. Everything else is a guess at the cautious end of what such a drive can do: rail acceleration 10 m/s², tilt 8 rad/s and 50 rad/s² with a 5:1 gearbox, focus 0.5 m/s and 10 m/s². The "moderate" case uses an earlier estimate, and the "fast" case is hypothetical. 10 bees, random flight.

  • Tracking is the same in all three cases, as it should be: the robot does not feed back into the tracker.

  • A wall the tracker does not know about breaks tracking. In the first run only the simulator knew the panel. The tracker's coverage rule wants half of the cameras that could see a place. Behind the panel half of them are blocked, so real bees were rejected: with 10 bees and random flight the worst bee was tracked only 39 % of the time, with 12 ghost tracks in the worst seed. This was found by the experiment and fixed: the tracker now reads the walls from the config and leaves blocked cameras out of the rule. The tables above are with that fix; the broken run is not reproduced by the current command.
  • With the fix the panel costs little with random flight (p95 1.32 mm instead of 1.06 mm, worst bee 97 % tracked). It costs more with trips: p95 2.48 mm instead of 1.27 mm, worst bee 88 %, and time in focus 66 % instead of 82 %.
  • Limit: the bees do not know the panel and fly through it. The numbers show the effect of lost views, not of bees crowding at an obstacle.

7What did not work or is weak

  • No layout with fewer than 16 cameras passed the criterion.
  • 8 cameras on 4 nodes are fragile: one failed node removes a corner edge and leaves the worst bee tracked 63 % of the time with trips.
  • Crowding (trips, 10 to 20 bees) causes most ID swaps and most ghost tracks of the correctly calibrated runs. The tracker needs to be better at telling apart bees that are close together, probably with appearance or a better motion model near walls.
  • Focus is the robot's weak point in every case: at most about a third of the time with random flight, even with fast limits. A 2 mm depth of field is very hard to hold on a bee that moves at 1 m/s.
  • The robot numbers vary by 10 to 25 points between seeds. Five seeds of 18 s are too few to rank layouts by them.
  • Prediction 100 ms ahead is poor with random flight (33 mm mean). This is a property of the random flight model as much as of the tracker.
  • Not run: the 8-camera layout on 8 nodes with a failed node.

8Assumptions and limits of the simulation

8.1 Assumptions the numbers depend on

  • Robot values are assumed. Only the rail speed of 3 m/s and the NEMA 23 motors are stated. The cinema picture of 24 mm and the depth of field of 2 mm are derived, not measured. Every robot number changes when Jeremy gives real values.
  • Both flight models are guesses. Neither is fitted to real bumblebee flight. The position of the feeder and the entrance in the trips model is my assumption, not from the video.
  • Camera noise and timing are the defaults of the camera-node specification (0.5 px, 2 % dropout, 8 ± 2 ms), not measured on a Raspberry Pi.
  • The robot emulator has no delay from stereo exposure to motor command beyond one frame, no vibration, no flex and a perfect focus curve.
  • The selected bee is always the oldest confirmed track, not a bee a person would pick.

8.2 What the simulation cannot show

  • Virtual cameras deliver points, not images: no reflections, no motion blur, no lighting, no background clutter and no blob detector. Whether a real camera finds a bee against the wall dots, or finds it at all in infrared, needs hardware.
  • Bees are points with a size; they do not hide each other, and nothing hides them (no occlusion, no robot in the view of a tracking camera).
  • The arena is a glass box. Opaque walls inside it only hide bees from cameras; bees fly through them.
  • The network is ideal apart from the configured delay and jitter: no lost datagrams other than dropout, no clock drift, a perfect trigger.
  • Each case was run for 18 evaluated seconds with 5 seeds: enough for tracking numbers, too few for robot numbers.

9What this means and what comes next

What the simulation supports. The concept works in the model. With 16 cameras and up to 5 bees the tracker keeps a stable ID per bee with about 1 mm accuracy, and the selected bee can be handed to a robot as a position, a velocity and a distance. It stays accurate under realistic noise, dropouts and delays, and a drifted calibration can be repaired from the wall markers alone.

What it does not support. It does not show that the robot would keep a macro lens on a bee. The focus numbers are poor in every case and rest on guessed motor values. And it says nothing about detecting a real bumblebee in a real image.

Questions for Jeremy Fielding (listed in the repository as the values to confirm):

  • Rail travel, maximum acceleration with the camera mounted, and the moving mass.
  • Tilt range, gear ratio, maximum speed and acceleration of the tilt axis.
  • Focus range and the speed of the focus motor.
  • Sensor size, magnification, aperture and the depth of field he actually works with.
  • Delay from the stereo camera to a motor command.
  • How many bees he wants in the arena at the same time, and where the colony entrance and the feeder are in relation to the light. This decides whether the random model or the trips model is closer.
  • Whether footage of the flights in the arena can be shared, so the flight model can be fitted.

Next steps.

  • Fit the flight model to real footage and repeat the experiments with real robot values.
  • Work on crowding: the weak point at 10 and more bees.
  • Stage 8 (optional): render simple camera images and run a real blob detector, so that reflections, wall dots and infrared lighting can be tested.
  • A small hardware test: two or four Raspberry Pi Global Shutter cameras on a table, checking triggering, detection and calibration with one real bee or a moving LED.
